FOLLOW-UP 2: STILL PRESENT ON BETA 4 (26A5388g). THE TRIGGER IS ANY OUTPUT-
DEVICE CHANGE, NOT A SPOTIFY/AIRPLAY TRANSFER. COREAUDIOD PAYS 1.5-1.8 CORES.

Caught live twice more on macOS 27.0 beta 4 (26A5388g), on 2026-08-04 and
2026-08-05, 15 and 16 days after that build shipped. The first of the two
ratchets in BOTH directions inside a single runaway; the second ran for 63
minutes and 107,675 writes, and shows the ratchet starting BEFORE any user
input. Both arrived through trigger paths that rule out the Spotify-specific
reading of my original report.

Three things in this comment are new. Two of them narrow the report; one of
them retracts a piece of evidence I supplied earlier.


1. THE TRIGGER IS AN OUTPUT-DEVICE CHANGE OF ANY KIND

My original report reached the runaway via a Spotify Connect transfer, which
makes it easy to read as a Spotify-specific interaction. It is not. This
occurrence had no Spotify transfer, no AirPlay hand-off, and no application
involvement at all: a pair of AirPods simply reconnected on their own after the
user walked away and came back, and the user pressed the volume-up key while
Control Center was still re-syncing to the newly-connected device.

A third occurrence on 2026-08-05 came through yet another path: an automatic
iPhone-to-Mac AirPods hand-off, logged as "show smart routing REVERSE ROUTE"
followed by configUpdateReasonEndedBottomUpRouteChange.

What the known occurrences share is therefore narrower and more general than
what I originally wrote: an output-device change immediately followed by a
volume mutation. The KIND of device change is irrelevant - Spotify Connect
transfer, unattended Bluetooth reconnect, and automatic iPhone-to-Mac hand-off
have each produced it. The window is short: 150 ms from the device-connect
banner to the key press, 20 ms from the key press to the first runaway write.


2. BOTH DIRECTIONS IN ONE RUNAWAY, AND FULL SCALE ON WORN HEADPHONES

Previous occurrences went one direction each. This one does both:

  11:02:15.360987  0.215000 -> 0.250000   first write
  11:02:15.930699  0.937500 -> 1.000000   FULL SCALE, 570 ms after onset
  ...              35 x 1.000000 -> 1.000000    spins at the top rail ~1.2 s
  11:02:17.099573  last write at 1.0, ratchet reverses
  11:02:19.996664  0.062500 -> 0.000000   bottom reached
  ...              4,604 x 0.000000 -> 0.000000  pinned + muted for 166 s
  11:05:06.031483  last write; recovered with "killall ControlCenter"

  Total: 4,849 "set system volume" writes over 170.7 s = 28.4/s.
  Step size is exactly 1/16 on all 208 real transitions.

On the hearing-safety point from my original report: this is the second
occurrence to drive a worn in-ear device to 1.000000, and it did so in 570 ms
with no ramp, on headphones the user had just put back on. Note which end the
user actually complained about - the stuck-at-zero tail, which is the harmless
one. The dangerous end happened first and lasted about a second. That asymmetry
is why this bug will be under-reported relative to its risk.


3. NEW: COREAUDIOD IS DRAGGED TO 150-181% CPU

My earlier reports measured Control Center's own cost. The larger cost is in
the HAL:

                    during runaway    after killall ControlCenter
  coreaudiod        150-181%          112% and falling
  ControlCenter     13.6-20.8%        0.3%

So the 30 Hz write loop costs roughly 1.5-1.8 cores in coreaudiod, an order of
magnitude more than in the agent issuing the writes, for as long as the runaway
lasts. The impact is not only that volume misbehaves; it is a sustained
system-wide CPU cost.


4. RETRACTION: THE NON-1/16 VALUES ON THE READ SIDE ARE NOT TORN READS

In my original report I offered, as supporting evidence for the race, that
non-1/16 values appear on the READ side of the ratchet (0.435000 -> 0.5,
0.490000 -> 0.5) while the write side only ever emits multiples of 1/16, and
suggested these were values that had been replaced between a thread's read and
its write. I rated that as weaker than my other points and noted the
alternative explanation.

The alternative is now confirmed and I am withdrawing the point. This
occurrence shows the same shape - 0.215000 on the read side - and the log
identifies where 0.215 comes from: "system volume of 22d updated: 0.562500 ->
0.215000" is Control Center adopting the AirPods' own stored volume when the
device connects, 169 ms before the runaway starts. These are accessory levels,
not torn reads. Please disregard that item.

The rest of the argument is unaffected and still stands:

  - the writes come from multiple concurrent ControlCenter threads;
  - the write side is always a clean 1/16 step from the value just written;
  - the loop self-sustains at ~30 Hz with no external input;
  - it terminates only when the agent is restarted.


5. ON THE THIRD-PARTY PRECONDITION

My follow-up established that Alcove 1.7.9 must be actively writing volume for
the runaway to fire, and that write pressure is not the variable (a synthetic
writer at 38x Alcove's rate, with Alcove quit, never triggered it). That still
holds - this occurrence is a sixth positive case.

But this occurrence also shows what Alcove does and does not do, in an incident
nobody staged. Alcove wrote once, 433 ms before onset, and Control Center
observed that value 2.4 ms later. Alcove then fell SILENT for the first 3.9
seconds of the runaway - through the entire climb to 1.000000, the top-rail
spin, and the reversal. Across the whole 170.7 s window: ControlCenter 9,947
client-side volume writes, Alcove 211, coreaudiod 5.

The self-sustaining 30 Hz loop is therefore entirely Control Center's own
writes. A third-party app can put the system into the state, but it is not
driving it and cannot stop it. Serializing volume mutations on a single
queue/actor inside Control Center removes the race regardless of what any other
process does; volume changes occur at human rates, so the serialization cost is
irrelevant.

6. THE RATCHET STARTS BEFORE ANY USER INPUT (2026-08-05, 63 MINUTES)

In my previous comment I flagged that I could not tell from logs whether the
user's volume adjustment is causally necessary or merely how the runaway gets
noticed. The 2026-08-05 occurrence answers half of that, and I would rather
supply the answer than let the correlation in my earlier reports be over-read.

  12:44:57.844  ControlCenter  system-banners: show smart routing REVERSE ROUTE
                               C4:B3:49:AA:E7:97        <- iPhone -> Mac hand-off
  12:45:06.594  Alcove         ['vmvc'] volume: 0.467500     ┐ four clean 1/16
  12:45:06.617  ControlCenter  system volume of 223 updated: │ steps, ~300 ms
                               0.530000 -> 0.467500          │ apart, NOT written
  12:45:06.894  Alcove         ['vmvc'] volume: 0.405000     │ by Control Center
  12:45:07.305  Alcove         ['vmvc'] volume: 0.342500     │ and with NO key

  12:45:08.146  AVRouting      configUpdateReasonEndedBottomUpRouteChange
  12:45:08.152  ControlCenter  system volume of 65 updated: 0.280000 -> 0.176838
  12:45:08.289  ControlCenter  [HotKeys] hot key 'volumeDown (builtin, down)'
  12:45:08.295  ControlCenter  <SoundSettings> (DeviceID 101) setLevel: 0.062500
  12:45:08.315  ControlCenter  set system volume: 0.176838 -> 0.062500  <- runaway

A descending 1/16 ratchet is therefore already running 1.7 SECONDS BEFORE the
only key event in the window. So the user's adjustment is not what starts it.

What I cannot claim: that the runaway reaches its self-sustaining state with
zero input. There is exactly one volumeDown key event, 26 ms before Control
Center's first runaway write, so input is present at the moment Control Center
takes over. Manual adjustment is not the initiator; it has not been excluded as
a contributor at that handover.

This occurrence also ran far longer than any previous one: 107,675 writes over
63 minutes at a sustained 1,800/min, ending only when I restarted the agent.


7. THE LOOP OUTLIVES THE DEVICE THAT TRIGGERED IT

During that 63-minute runaway the AirPods disconnected and the system fell back
to the built-in speakers. Control Center kept writing at 30 Hz on the built-in
device for the rest of the hour. Once established, the loop depends neither on
the third-party app (section 5) nor on the audio endpoint whose arrival
triggered it. Only restarting the agent stops it.

One practical note for anyone reproducing: on this occurrence, restarting
Control Center stopped the write loop immediately (0 writes in the following
5 s) but did NOT restore the volume - it stayed at 0 and muted until raised by
hand. On the 2026-08-04 occurrence it did come back on its own. Stopping the
runaway and recovering the volume are separate outcomes.
